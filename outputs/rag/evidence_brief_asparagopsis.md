# Asparagopsis: distinguish beef and dairy evidence

Source check: **4 September 2026**. This is a curated snapshot of two selected
PubMed-indexed studies, based on their abstracts. It is not a systematic review,
an exhaustive update, or a live search.

## What the selected studies report

- **Roque et al. (2021), beef cattle:** Twenty-one Angus-Hereford steers were
  studied over 147 days. Across the experimental period, methane yield fell by
  45% and 68% in the two supplemented groups. Responses varied with forage
  context. The higher treatment reduced dry-matter intake; average daily gain
  did not differ. These are beef-steer results, not dairy-cow estimates.
  [PubMed record, PMID 33730064](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33730064/).
- **Stefenoni et al. (2021), dairy cattle:** A 20-cow Latin-square experiment
  found lower methane in the higher seaweed treatment during the first two
  periods, but not the last two. The authors linked this change to declining
  bromoform content during storage. That treatment also reduced intake and milk
  yield and increased milk iodine and bromide concentrations.
  [PubMed record, PMID 33516546](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33516546/).

## Interpretation and limits

Animal category, diet, seaweed composition, storage and production outcomes
matter. A large reduction reported in one setting is not a dependable response
for every farm, and methane reduction alone does not establish an overall
production benefit or product safety.

**Connection to this app:** The generic `seaweed` category does not describe a
specific species, batch, active-compound concentration or physical dose. Its
model response cannot represent all Asparagopsis products. The saved model has
not been validated here against these studies.

Read the full papers and assess safety, feed quality and local requirements
with qualified specialists before practical use. No feeding dose is recommended.
