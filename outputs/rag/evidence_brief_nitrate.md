# Nitrate: methane response and safety endpoints

Source check: **4 September 2026**. This is a curated snapshot of two selected
PubMed-indexed studies, based on their abstracts. It is not a systematic review,
an exhaustive update, or a live search.

## What the selected studies report

- **van Zijderveld et al. (2011):** Twenty lactating cows were followed over
  four 24-day periods after adaptation. Compared with the urea control, nitrate
  produced a sustained reduction of about 16% in methane production. The saved
  energy did not improve milk production or energy balance. Blood methemoglobin
  was higher in the nitrate group.
  [PubMed record, PMID 21787938](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21787938/).
- **Olijhoek et al. (2016):** A four-cow Latin-square study tested four dietary
  nitrate levels. Methane measured as volume per unit of dry-matter intake
  declined with increasing nitrate. Intake and milk yield did not change.
  Blood methemoglobin and nitrate concentrations in milk and urine increased;
  the authors did not identify a safety threat under their experimental conditions.
  [PubMed record, PMID 27236758](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27236758/).

## Interpretation and limits

Both experiments support a methane response under controlled conditions, with
important differences in design and a very small sample in the second study.
The safety observations cannot be generalized to unsupervised feeding, different
adaptation procedures, or different diets.

**Connection to this app:** The `nitrate` category and normalized dose do not
model methemoglobin risk, adaptation, product composition or a physical feeding
dose. The sensitivity table must not be used to choose a nitrate dose. The saved
model has not been validated here against these studies.

Any practical consideration requires qualified animal-nutrition and veterinary
assessment. This brief provides literature context, not a feeding recommendation.
